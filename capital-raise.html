Raising capital: a practical playbook for your seed round

A capital raise is a project with a start, a deadline and a pipeline. Founders who treat it that way raise faster and on better terms than founders who treat it as a series of meetings. Here is the playbook, from deciding how much to raise to closing.

Decide how much — and why

Start from milestones, not a headline number. Ask: what must be true for the next round to be easy? Revenue, retention, a product shipped, a market opened. Then work out the team and time it takes to get there.

  • Milestones — the two or three results that define the next round.
  • Runway — commonly planned as 18 to 24 months, so you raise again from strength, not urgency.
  • Buffer — things take longer than planned. Budget for it.

The result is a range you can defend in one sentence: "We're raising €1.5M to reach €1M ARR and launch in two new markets over 20 months."

Choose the instrument

Early rounds are usually raised through either a priced equity round or a convertible instrument that turns into equity later. Which is available — and which is customary — depends on your jurisdiction and company form.

InstrumentWhat it isConsider when
Priced equity roundShares issued at an agreed valuationYou have a lead investor and want certainty on ownership
Convertible note or loanDebt that converts into equity at the next roundYou want speed and to defer the valuation discussion
SAFE-style agreementA right to future equity, without debt termsIt is standard where you are incorporated — common in the US, less so in much of Europe

This is an overview, not legal advice. Instruments, tax treatment and investor protections vary by country. Agree terms with a lawyer who closes startup rounds in your jurisdiction.

Prepare before the first meeting

  • A short deck — problem, customer, product, traction, team, the raise and what it buys.
  • Your numbers — revenue or usage, growth, retention, burn and runway, defined consistently.
  • A data room — cap table, incorporation documents, key contracts, financials, and the metrics behind the deck.
  • An investor pipeline — a qualified list of investors whose ticket, stage, thesis and geography fit. See how to build one.

Run a tight process

Momentum is the most valuable asset in a raise. Investors move faster when they see others moving.

  1. Batch first meetings. Schedule them close together so conversations progress in parallel, not one after another.
  2. Find the lead first. Followers wait for a lead. Prioritise investors who lead at your stage.
  3. Keep first meetings short. Twenty minutes to establish fit; save the deep dive for those who want one. See how to run them.
  4. Track every investor by stage — first meeting, follow-up, partner meeting, term sheet, passed — with the reason for each pass.
  5. Update everyone on real progress: a new customer, a lead committed, a closing date.

Close the round

  • Negotiate the term sheet with your lead, then share final terms with followers.
  • Set a closing date and keep to it; open-ended rounds lose momentum.
  • Prepare for confirmatory due diligence — a tidy data room makes this quick.
  • Announce once the money is in the bank, not before.

Questions founders ask

How much should a startup raise in a seed round?

Enough to reach the milestones that make the next round possible, plus a buffer — commonly planned as 18 to 24 months of runway. Work backwards from milestones and monthly spend.

How long does a seed capital raise take?

Preparation takes weeks; the active process works best compressed, with first meetings close together so conversations progress in parallel. Closing paperwork adds time after a term sheet.

What do investors expect to see in a seed round?

A clear problem and customer, early evidence such as revenue, usage or pilots, a credible team, how much you're raising and what it will achieve, and a tidy data room.
