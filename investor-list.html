The investor list that actually fits your raise

An investor list is only as good as its fit. Most founders start with hundreds of names and discover, one follow-up at a time, that the fund doesn't do their stage, their ticket or their country. This guide shows how to build a short list that holds up — and when to stop building lists at all.

What an investor list is for

An investor list is the set of funds, angels and partners you plan to approach for a specific round. Its job is not to be long. Its job is to make sure that every conversation you start has a real chance of ending in a cheque: the investor writes your ticket size, invests at your stage, covers your sector and your geography, and is deploying now.

A list built on names alone fails quietly. You spend weeks on outreach, the replies that come back are polite passes, and the reason — "we don't do pre-seed", "we only lead", "we're not investing outside the DACH region" — was knowable before you wrote the first email.

The fields that decide fit

For every entry, capture the facts that would end the conversation if they don't match. Everything else is secondary.

FieldWhy it matters
Partner, not just fundFunds don't take meetings; partners do. Note the one person whose focus matches yours.
Ticket size rangeA fund writing €1–3M cheques can't do a €200k angel ticket, and vice versa.
StagesPre-seed, seed and Series A are different businesses for an investor.
Thesis and sectorsLook for the stated thesis and recent deals, not a long list of logos.
GeographyMany funds only invest in specific countries or require a local entity.
Lead or followYou need a lead before followers commit. Know which is which.
Actively deployingRecent investments are the best evidence a fund still has capital to invest.
Intro pathWho can introduce you — a portfolio founder, an angel, a mutual contact.

Where to find investors

  • Founders one or two years ahead of you. Ask who invested, who was helpful, and who said no and why. This is the most reliable source of fit you will get.
  • Fund websites and portfolio pages. The thesis page and the last ten portfolio companies tell you more than any database row.
  • Public investor databases. Good for breadth; always verify the ticket and stage, because records go stale.
  • Angel networks and syndicates. Often the right first cheques for pre-seed and seed rounds.
  • Accelerators and demo days. Investors who attend are, by definition, looking at your stage.
  • Announcements of comparable rounds. Who backed the companies one step ahead of you in your sector?

How to qualify each name

Before a name stays on the list, answer four questions with evidence, not assumptions:

  1. Has this investor made an investment at your stage and ticket size recently?
  2. Does your company sit inside their written thesis — or would they have to stretch?
  3. Do they invest in your country, with your company structure?
  4. Is there a portfolio conflict — a direct competitor they already back?

If you can't answer the first question, the name isn't qualified yet. If the answer to the last one is yes, take it off the list.

Rule of thumb: a shorter list you can fully qualify beats a long one you can't. Every unqualified name costs you an email, a follow-up and some momentum.

Turning the list into a pipeline

Once qualified, sort the list into tiers and run it as a pipeline, not a mailing list:

  • Tier 1 — likely leads with a strong thesis fit and a warm intro path.
  • Tier 2 — good fit, weaker intro path, or likely followers.
  • Tier 3 — plausible fit you still need to confirm.

Approach tiers close together so conversations progress in parallel. Track each investor's status — contacted, first meeting, follow-up, partner meeting, passed — and the reason for every pass. Those reasons are the best feedback you'll get on your list and your pitch.

Questions founders ask

How many investors should be on a seed investor list?

Enough that you can run a short, parallel process with the investors who genuinely fit — usually a few dozen qualified names rather than hundreds of unqualified ones. Fit matters more than count.

What should an investor list include?

For each investor: the partner's name, ticket size range, stages, sectors in their thesis, geographies, whether they lead or follow, evidence they are actively investing, a relevant portfolio company, and your intro path.

Is an investor list enough to raise a round?

No. A list gives you names; you still have to qualify each one, find an introduction and earn a meeting. Matching tools such as Binder skip the list step and connect both sides only when both say yes.
