Start with fit, not access

Most fundraising advice is about getting in the room. But a meeting with an investor who doesn't write your ticket, doesn't invest at your stage or doesn't cover your market is a meeting that can't end in a yes — however good the pitch.

Before you try to meet anyone, confirm the basics: ticket size, stage, sector thesis, geography, and whether they lead or follow. Our investor list guide covers how to check each of these.

Getting a warm introduction

A warm intro works because someone the investor trusts is vouching for the fit. Make that easy:

  • Ask the right person. A founder the investor backed carries more weight than a loose LinkedIn connection.
  • Send a forwardable note. Three or four sentences: what you do, one proof point, what you're raising, why this investor specifically.
  • Ask first, then introduce. A good introducer checks the investor wants the intro before making it. That's a feature — it means the meeting starts with interest on both sides.

The 20-minute first meeting

The first meeting isn't the pitch marathon. Its job is to establish fit and agree a next step. A structure that works:

  1. 5 minutes — the company. The problem, who pays, traction since you started, and what you're raising.
  2. 10 minutes — the investor's fit. Their thesis, how they work with founders, whether they lead, what they'd need to see.
  3. 5 minutes — next steps. Data room, a partner meeting, references, a timeline.

Come with questions. Ask how decisions are made, how long they usually take, and what would make this a no. You learn more from those answers than from their reaction to your deck.

After the meeting

  • Follow up the same day with anything you promised — the deck, a metric, a customer reference.
  • Ask for the next step explicitly, with a date.
  • Record the outcome and the reason. A clear pass is useful; silence usually means no.
  • Keep investors who passed for timing reasons informed with a short update when something meaningful changes.

Questions founders ask

What is the best way to meet investors as a first-time founder?

A warm introduction from someone the investor trusts — usually a founder they backed — to an investor whose ticket, stage and thesis already fit your company. Fit first, then the introduction.

Does cold emailing investors work?

Sometimes, when the email is short, specific and sent to an investor whose thesis clearly fits. It works far less often than warm introductions, and worst when sent to a long, unqualified list.

How long should a first investor meeting be?

Short. Twenty to thirty minutes is enough to establish fit and agree a next step. Binder books 20-minute coffee chats by default.
